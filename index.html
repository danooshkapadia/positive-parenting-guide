A family guide · Learning together

Parenting with warmth & clear limits.

A practical introduction to Positive Discipline, for the everyday moments when parenting gets hard.

Read the guideFind the next step

A child will not stop watching, put on shoes, or get ready for bed. We repeat ourselves. Our voices rise. We reach for a threat, and soon the argument is bigger than the task.

Positive Discipline offers another way through: help children feel connected, keep expectations clear, and teach the skills they need to meet them. The goal is growing cooperation and responsibility over time.

The examples follow Shyla and Danoosh, with Ori (11) and Zahan (4). They are situations to learn from and adapt, not judgments about anyone in the family.

Start small, together.

Read once when things are calm. Pick one situation and one change to practice. Look for a steadier response, a clearer plan, or an easier recovery. Immediate agreement is not the only sign of progress.

02 The ideas underneath

Children need connection and guidance.

Positive Discipline treats discipline as teaching. Jane Nelsen developed the approach from Adlerian ideas about belonging, contribution, and mutual respect. Parents lead; children also need to feel that they matter and can take part.

Kindness and firmness can happen together.

Kindness takes the feeling seriously. Firmness keeps a reasonable boundary clear. Understanding disappointment does not require changing the answer.

Look beyond the visible action.

“Won’t stop” tells us what happened, but not why it was difficult. A child may need help with the transition, a clearer plan, rest, or a meaningful choice. Ask and observe rather than deciding you already know.

What we can see

“I’m not turning it off!”

A hard transition

Wanting a say

Help the child notice their own progress.

Encouragement points to effort, contribution, or a skill: “You stopped even though it was hard.” This gives the child something concrete to recognize and repeat. Warm affection still belongs; you do not need to monitor every word of praise.

A 30-second rehearsal for both parents

One parent says, “But I want more time!” The other acknowledges the feeling and states the limit in one or two sentences. Switch roles.

Notice how it feels to hear understanding without having the boundary disappear. Adjust the words until they sound natural.

03 Before · Prepare

Make success easier before the difficult moment.

A clear plan gives a child less to work out while disappointed or rushed. Preparation will not prevent every protest. It gives both parents and children something familiar to return to.

Build the ending into the beginning.

1Name the plan

Explain the stopping point before watching starts.

Words to try“One episode, then the iPad goes away.”

2Give a reminder

Get close enough to be heard. Link the reminder to the agreed ending.

Words to try“This episode is nearly finished.”

3Make the next step clear

Use an ordinary part of the routine, rather than offering a new prize.

Words to try“Then we’ll choose our bedtime book.”

With Zahan

Use a short sequence and pictures when useful. Rehearse the steps at a calm time. Offer a choice about how to move forward: which book, or who carries it.

With Ori

Agree on a realistic stopping point together, including time to save a game. Ask which reminder would help. Leave room for responsibility and privacy.

Connection also happens outside conflict.

Spend some ordinary, undivided time with each child: talking, playing, or doing something together. It is part of the relationship, not something they have to earn by behaving well.

Keep routines small. A few clear steps are easier to practice than a long list. “When shoes are on, we go outside” describes a sequence. “If you stop crying, you get a treat” makes managing feelings a condition for a reward.

04 Before → During

A different screen-time ending.

The screen still turns off. What changes is how we prepare for the ending, respond to disappointment, and help the child move on.

The familiar escalation

1. Abrupt stop

“Show’s over.” We grab the device or remote and turn it off.

2. Upset grows

The child protests. We feel pushed and repeat ourselves more forcefully.

3. A bigger threat

“If you can’t behave, no screen time tomorrow.”

Now the argument includes both today’s ending and tomorrow’s punishment. Try changing the sequence while keeping the original limit.

A clear ending, with support

First explain the stopping point and give a reminder, as on the preparation page. When the ending arrives:

This is not a promise of no tears.

The better approach is preparation, a steady boundary, and support. If feelings grow, use fewer words and keep everyone safe. Avoid a tug-of-war over the device.

What to do if it stays difficult →
Adapt the example for Ori

Plan a stopping point that allows saving or finishing an agreed short step. At the ending, acknowledge the frustration without adding a new penalty. Later, ask whether the reminder or stopping point needs adjusting.

05 During · Support

Make the next step smaller.

When voices rise and bodies tense, a long explanation can add more to cope with. Start with safety and a steadier response. The conversation about what happened can wait.

If both choices are refused

Do not keep adding choices or penalties. State what you will do, pause if it is safe, and help with the next necessary step. At a dangerous edge, safety comes first; do not wait for agreement.

If there is hitting or throwing

Move people or objects to safety. Keep your words brief: “I won’t let you hit. I’m moving back.” Offer quiet company or space; do not insist on a hug.

Parents can hand over, too.

Agree on a simple phrase such as “Can you take over for a minute?” Pause while the children remain safely supervised. Discuss differences later when possible, while still speaking up if a response is unsafe or unkind.

06 After · Learn

Get curious. Make one plan together.

Getting through today’s ending is only part of the work. Once everyone is ready to think, return to the problem. Children can help discover a solution that adults might miss.

Everyone can contribute. The adults still hold the boundary.

1 Listen

“We need screen time to end. What makes stopping hard?” Hear both perspectives.

2 Choose

Collect ideas. Choose one small change that is workable for everyone.

3 Try

Practice the new ending. Give it a few real attempts.

4 Notice

“What helped? What was still hard?” Adjust the plan.

↶ Use what you noticed to prepare the next attempt.

Keep the conversation manageable.

Ori can help define the reminder and stopping point. With Zahan, keep it brief and offer two ideas or pictures. Neither child has to explain perfectly.

Repair before solving.

If you lost your temper, own that part without blaming the child. The limit can remain while you change how you handle it.

Words to try“I shouted. I’m sorry. I want to handle that more calmly.”
Try a five-minute parent debrief

Ask each other: What was hard? What helped? What will we both try next time? Agree on one phrase and one action. Keep it focused on improving the plan, not grading each other.

07 Choose a helpful response

What does this teach?

A consequence is what follows an action. Some happen naturally; others are arranged by an adult. The useful question is whether the response helps the child learn or simply makes the mistake cost more.

One event: a drink spills.

↓ Compare what follows

The natural result

The table is wet.

It happens without an adult adding anything. A natural consequence is not a reason to allow danger or withhold needed help.

A related response

“Let’s get a cloth.”

Cleaning together addresses the spill and teaches a skill. An adult-arranged, connected response is often called a logical consequence.

An unrelated penalty

“No TV tonight.”

Removing TV does not teach how to manage a spill. The extra penalty can distract from the problem that needs solving.

Four tests for an adult’s response

A response needs all four. Being related is not enough if the response is humiliating or excessive.

Related

Does it connect directly to what happened?

Respectful

Can we do it without blame, shame, or pain?

Reasonable

Does it fit the child’s ability and the situation?

Helpful

Does it teach a skill or help solve the problem?

Often, change the setup.

If spilling keeps happening, practice pouring or use a smaller jug. If cleanup keeps failing, make the job smaller or storage easier. A clear routine and help getting started may do more than an added consequence.

Explain expectations in advance whenever possible. Keep food separate from behavior, and never allow danger or harm to “teach a lesson.”

08 Transfer it to daily life

Same principles. Different moments.

Choose one situation first. Notice the move from a threat to a manageable action, then a plan that helps next time.

Cleanup

Usual response: “Put everything away or you lose dessert.”

During → Help now
Words to try“The blocks go in the bin. I’ll start with you.”

Give Zahan one clear part of the job. A shared start can make an overwhelming task smaller.

After → Prepare next time

Ask Ori what gets in the way. Agree on a small task and a time. With Zahan, try picture labels or fewer things out.

The lesson: make the task doable, then practice responsibility.

Bedtime

Usual response: “No story if you don’t listen!”

During → Help now
Words to try“It’s time to get ready. Teeth or pajamas first?”

Both choices move toward the same routine. If choosing is too much, offer help with one step.

After → Prepare next time

Make a short routine together: pictures for Zahan, a simple checklist for Ori if useful. Practice when no one is rushed.

The lesson: offer agency inside a clear boundary.

Sibling conflict

Usual response: “Both of you stop, or I’m taking everything away.”

During → Help now
Words to try“I’m going to help you move apart so everyone is safe.”

If bodies are unsafe, separate and help each child settle. Hear each account when they can talk.

After → Prepare next time

Find out what each child needed. Practice asking for a turn, protecting a project, or getting an adult’s help.

The lesson: safety first, then teach the missing skill.

09 Keep handy

What is the next helpful step?

Start with safety. You only need to choose the next move, not solve the whole pattern right now.

Is anyone unsafe?

Move people or objects to safety. Stay nearby and keep words brief.

Are feelings running high?

Keep the limit simple. Offer space or company. Stay here longer if needed.

Ready to think together?

Ask what was hard, choose one small change, and practice it next time.

See the whole map

Unsafe → protect. Move people or objects to safety; do not wait for agreement.

Safe, but overwhelmed → support. Use fewer words, stay available, and hold the simple limit. This stage may take time.

Ready to talk → learn. Hear both perspectives and choose a doable plan. If upset grows again, return to support.

10 Keep exploring

Questions you may have.

Is this being permissive?

The limit remains. Kindness changes how you hold it: you can acknowledge the feeling, offer help, and follow through. On the screen-time page, the iPad stays off even while the child is disappointed.

What if it seems harder this week?

Difficulty alone does not tell you whether the approach is working. Look at sleep, timing, demands, the clarity of the plan, and the support available. Make one manageable adjustment.

If the pattern remains intense, unsafe, or disruptive despite support, seek tailored help from a pediatrician or qualified parenting professional.

Do both parents need to use identical words?

No. Agree on the boundary and how you will help. Each parent can sound like themselves. A short shared phrase can be useful under stress, but consistency is about the plan and response, not performing a script perfectly.

What if we already threatened a punishment?

You can correct your own response: “I said no screens tomorrow because I was upset. I’m changing that. Screen time is still finished for today.” Then return later to a plan you can follow through on respectfully.
